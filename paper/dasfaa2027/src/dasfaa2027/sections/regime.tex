\section{The Controlling Variable Is the Seed Fraction}
\label{sec:regime}

\subsection{Why absolute budgets mislead}

Our first two attempts to characterise this problem produced the conclusion that out-degree
\emph{does not} fail under overexposure, and both were wrong for the same reason. We sampled
candidate nodes uniformly from the whole graph and correlated out-degree with the true marginal
gain. On a heavy-tailed graph such a pool consists almost entirely of low-degree nodes, so the
correlation is dominated by the global regularity ``high-degree nodes are more valuable'' rather
than by any ability to rank candidates under a given state. Worse, the experiments were run at
tiny coverage: on NetHEPT ($n = 15233$) a budget of $200$ seeds is $1.3\%$ of the network.

Replacing the uniform pool by a degree-retrieved pool --- which is what a deployed
\emph{retrieve-then-rerank} pipeline actually sees --- moves the measured out-degree rank
correlation from $0.882$ to $0.654$ on the same graph and objective, because the pool no longer
spans the degree distribution.

The decisive variable, however, is not the pool but the \emph{seed fraction} $|S|/n$. Saturation
is a property of coverage, not of the absolute budget. The same budget of $200$ seeds is $1.3\%$
of NetHEPT but $42\%$ of Congress-Twitter ($n = 475$).

\subsection{Sweeping the fraction across nine graphs}

We therefore sweep $|S|/n \in \{0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.40\}$ on nine graphs whose mean degree
spans $4.23$ to $55.95$, and measure the rank correlation between out-degree and the true
marginal gain $\Delta(v \mid S) = \sigma(S \cup \{v\}) - \sigma(S)$, estimated by paired Monte
Carlo with windows shared across candidates within a trial.

Three observations follow from Table~\ref{tab:signflip}. They are stated as hypotheses to be
re-measured, not as results: the table is withdrawn (see its caption), and a re-measurement at
$\text{MC} = 300$ under the fixed model already contradicts observation 2 on Congress-Twitter,
where the share of negative marginals falls from $44\%$ to $4\%$ and no candidate is negative
beyond noise.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Out-degree flips sign.} It is positively correlated with the marginal gain on
        only one of nine graphs --- NetHEPT, the sparsest --- and negative on the other eight
        once $|S|/n \ge 20\%$.
  \item \textbf{The objective really is non-monotone there.} \withdrawn{} Between $8\%$ and $35\%$
        of candidates have a \emph{negative} marginal gain on the denser graphs. This was offered
        as matching the source model's own report that the objective value fluctuates with the
        budget on its sparsest dataset; the re-measurement suggests most of the share was
        estimation noise, so the phenomenon must be established at $\text{MC} \ge 300$ with paired
        per-trial differences before it can be claimed.
  \item \textbf{The state-conditioned closed form stays positive on all nine.} It is the only
        scorer we tested that does. \withdrawn{} The re-measurement gives $\rho_{\delta_2} = +0.030$
        on Congress-Twitter at $|S|/n = 20\%$ and $\rho_{\text{degree}} = +0.039$ in the same cell,
        i.e.\ no separation; the claim that $\delta_2$ is a usable ranker in this regime is not
        supported by the re-derived numbers.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Density is not the explanation.}
It is tempting to attribute a sign flip to density. We do not, for two reasons. First, the claim was
never supported even by the numbers we had: the rank correlation between $\langle k \rangle$ and
$\rho_{\text{degree}}$ was only weakly negative, and Congress-Twitter, the densest graph, was a
counterexample at $\rho_{\text{degree}} = -0.049$. Second, and more seriously, \withdrawn{} both
components of that correlation were measured at $\text{MC} = 12$ (\S\ref{sec:experiments}), so even
its sign is currently unknown; the re-measurement under the fixed model gives
$\rho_{\text{degree}} = +0.039$ on Congress-Twitter at $|S|/n = 20\%$, where the withdrawn table
records $-0.112$. The defensible claim, when there is one, will be about the \emph{saturated regime}
and will be supported by paired per-trial differences at $\text{MC} \ge 300$, not by a correlation
against density.

\paragraph{A structural hint.}
After in-weight normalisation, exposure $\delta$ is driven by a node's \emph{in}-degree while a
seed's value is driven by its \emph{out}-degree. The two coincide only when a graph is
approximately symmetric. Consistently, the graphs with $\rho(\text{in},\text{out})$ near $1$
(ca-GrQc and ca-HepPh, both symmetrised collaboration networks, and Trust Bitcoin-Alpha at
$+0.862$) show strongly negative out-degree correlations, while NetHEPT, whose in- and
out-degrees are nearly independent ($\rho = -0.051$), is the one graph where degree survives.
We record this as a hypothesis rather than a result; testing it needs more graphs than the nine
available here.
