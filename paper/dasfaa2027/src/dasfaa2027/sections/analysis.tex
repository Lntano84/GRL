\section{A Training-Free State-Conditioned Score}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Definition}

We need a scorer that (i) requires no sampling, and (ii) reads the \emph{realised} exposure
state. Observation~\ref{obs:setfunction} makes the state cheap to obtain: given the windows,
one cascade returns $\delta(v)$ for every $v$, at a cost of one simulation per decision rather
than one per candidate.

Let $g(e) = 2e(1-e)$ by Remark~\ref{rem:lemma1}, and let $N^+(w)$ be the out-neighbours of a
candidate $w$, each with weight $\omega_{wv}$. Adding $w$ raises the exposure of each
$v \in N^+(w)$ by
\begin{equation}
  \Delta e_w(v) \;=\; \omega_{wv}\,\bigl(1 - \delta(v)\bigr),
  \label{eq:delta-e}
\end{equation}
the factor $(1-\delta(v))$ accounting for the influence $v$ has already accumulated. The score
of $w$ is the resulting change in the expected number of its positively active out-neighbours,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{score}(w) \;=\; \sum_{v \in N^+(w)} \Bigl[\, g\bigl(\delta(v) + \Delta e_w(v)\bigr)
  \;-\; g\bigl(\delta(v)\bigr) \Bigr].
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
We take two hops: \eqref{eq:delta-e} is propagated once more through the out-edges of each $v$,
each hop attenuated by $(1-\delta(\cdot))$, before \eqref{eq:score} is evaluated. We refer to
\eqref{eq:score} as $\delta_2$.

Two properties matter for what follows. First, \eqref{eq:score} is a \emph{closed form}: no
Monte-Carlo trials are involved, so its decisions carry no estimation noise. Second, because
$g$ is concave and non-monotone, \eqref{eq:score} can be \emph{negative} --- it penalises a
candidate that would push already-exposed neighbours past their overexposure threshold. A
negative score expresses ``do not seed this'', which is exactly the information that is absent
from a degree ranking.

\subsection{Why a closed form can beat a noisy exact method}

\eqref{eq:score} is not an unbiased estimator of $\Delta(w \mid S)$; empirically it
under-estimates the marginal by roughly two orders of magnitude at $|S|/n = 0$ and its sign can
differ from the truth once saturated. That sounds disqualifying and is not, for a reason that is
central to this paper.

Once the network saturates, the true marginal $\Delta(w \mid S)$ is close to zero for
\emph{every} candidate. The spread of the true marginals across candidates therefore also
collapses. A method that estimates the marginal by Monte Carlo --- which is what exact greedy
does --- then faces a signal-to-noise problem: the differences it must resolve are smaller than
its own estimation error, so its argmax is driven by noise. A method that is \emph{biased but
noise-free} can still rank usefully, and --- as \S\ref{sec:experiments} shows --- does.

This is not a claim that bias is harmless. It is a claim about which error dominates in this
specific regime, and we quantify it in \S\ref{sec:experiments}.

The practical consequence, visible in Table~\ref{tab:calibration}: the \emph{zero crossing} of
$\delta_2$ is not an unbiased estimate of the point at which adding seeds stops helping.
An early version of our pipeline used ``stop when $\mathrm{score} \le 0$'' as a stopping rule
and halted after a single seed, scoring $347$ against a degree baseline of $364$. Stopping
decisions must therefore come from the observed spread --- which is exact, by
Observation~\ref{obs:setfunction} --- rather than from the analytic score. We use a
patience-based stop on the observed spread in \S\ref{sec:experiments}.
