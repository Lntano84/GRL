\section{The Controlling Variable Is the Seed Fraction}
\label{sec:regime}

\subsection{Why absolute budgets mislead}

Our first two attempts to characterise this problem produced the conclusion that out-degree
\emph{does not} fail under overexposure, and both were wrong for the same reason. We sampled
candidate nodes uniformly from the whole graph and correlated out-degree with the true marginal
gain. On a heavy-tailed graph such a pool consists almost entirely of low-degree nodes, so the
correlation is dominated by the global regularity ``high-degree nodes are more valuable'' rather
than by any ability to rank candidates under a given state. Worse, the experiments were run at
tiny coverage: on NetHEPT ($n = 15233$) a budget of $200$ seeds is $1.3\%$ of the network.

Replacing the uniform pool by a degree-retrieved pool --- which is what a deployed
\emph{retrieve-then-rerank} pipeline actually sees --- moves the measured out-degree rank
correlation from $0.882$ to $0.654$ on the same graph and objective, because the pool no longer
spans the degree distribution.

The decisive variable, however, is not the pool but the \emph{seed fraction} $|S|/n$. Saturation
is a property of coverage, not of the absolute budget. The same budget of $200$ seeds is $1.3\%$
of NetHEPT but $42\%$ of Congress-Twitter ($n = 475$).

\subsection{Sweeping the fraction across eight graphs}

We therefore sweep $|S|/n \in \{0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.40\}$ on eight graphs whose mean degree
spans $4.23$ to $55.95$, and measure the rank correlation between out-degree and the true
marginal gain $\Delta(v \mid S) = \sigma(S \cup \{v\}) - \sigma(S)$, estimated by paired Monte
Carlo with windows shared across candidates within a trial.

Three observations follow from Table~\ref{tab:signflip}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Out-degree flips sign.} It is positively correlated with the marginal gain on
        only one of eight graphs --- NetHEPT, the sparsest --- and negative on the other seven
        once $|S|/n \ge 20\%$.
  \item \textbf{The objective really is non-monotone there.} Between $20\%$ and $35\%$ of
        candidates have a \emph{negative} marginal gain on the denser graphs. This matches the
        source model's own report that the objective value fluctuates with the budget on its
        sparsest dataset.
  \item \textbf{The state-conditioned closed form stays positive on all eight.} It is the only
        scorer we tested that does.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Density is not the explanation.}
It is tempting to attribute the sign flip to density. We do not, because the evidence does not
support it: the rank correlation between $\langle k \rangle$ and $\rho_{\text{degree}}$ across
the eight graphs is only $-0.405$, and Congress-Twitter --- the densest graph --- is a
counterexample at $\rho_{\text{degree}} = -0.049$. The defensible claim is about the
\emph{saturated regime}, not about dense graphs.

\paragraph{A structural hint.}
After in-weight normalisation, exposure $\delta$ is driven by a node's \emph{in}-degree while a
seed's value is driven by its \emph{out}-degree. The two coincide only when a graph is
approximately symmetric. Consistently, the graphs with $\rho(\text{in},\text{out})$ near $1$
(ca-GrQc and ca-HepPh, both symmetrised collaboration networks) show the strongest negative
out-degree correlation, while NetHEPT, whose in- and out-degrees are nearly independent
($\rho = -0.051$), is the one graph where degree survives. We record this as a hypothesis rather
than a result; testing it needs more graphs than the eight available here.
