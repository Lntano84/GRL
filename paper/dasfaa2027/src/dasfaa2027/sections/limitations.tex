\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

We list the things that could be wrong, in the order we consider them most likely to matter.

\paragraph{1. A learned scorer did not beat the closed form, in a study we must re-run.}
\withdrawn{} The natural question after establishing a training-free baseline is whether a learned
state-conditioned scorer can beat it. On the evidence we collected it could not. We built a dataset
of $480$ labelled states on Congress-Twitter ($4$ seed fractions $\times$ $3$ independent contexts
$\times$ $40$ candidates, paired labels at $\text{MC} = 400$) and evaluated with
\emph{leave-one-context-out}, so every score is a cross-state generalisation score. With
$\mathrm{regret} = 1 - (\text{model top-}5\text{ gain}) / (\text{true top-}5\text{ gain})$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
model & Spearman & regret \\
\midrule
$\delta_2$ (no training) & $\mathbf{+0.6932}$ & $\mathbf{0.5025}$ \\
ridge on the same features & $+0.4255$ & $0.6172$ \\
MLP on the same features & $+0.3652$ & $0.9662$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A feature ablation, replicated on two independently generated datasets, shows that this is not a
matter of insufficient model capacity. Adding threshold-derived features (distance to
$\theta^\tau$, fraction of neighbours inside the band) to the analytic score makes held-out
regret \emph{worse} on both replications ($+0.167$ and $+0.300$), and on the first replication
the combination wins $0/12$ held-out contexts. The single analytic score column is the strongest
single predictor we found.

This should be read as a limitation, not as a theorem: $480$ states is small, and we cannot
exclude that a larger corpus would change the answer. What we can say is that under a
cross-state evaluation protocol, at this scale, the closed form was not beaten, and the
threshold features --- which were our main hypothesis for where extra signal would come from ---
were actively unhelpful. We report it because a paper that shows a strong training-free baseline
should also report that learning did not improve on it. \withdrawn{} The labels themselves came
from the pre-fix state machine, so both the absolute regrets and the sign of the threshold-feature
effect must be re-measured before this paragraph can claim anything; what is unaffected is the
methodological warning that a pooled feature-importance statistic here measures regime
identification rather than utility.

\paragraph{2. A trap in interpreting feature importance here.}
\withdrawn{} Pooled across contexts, \emph{every} informative feature correlated with the label at
$|r| \approx 0.85$--$0.91$, including one at $0.907$ that is pure regime identification. This is
because the dataset mixes regimes whose label scales differ by three orders of magnitude
($\approx +200$ when unsaturated, $\approx 0$ when saturated). Any evaluation that does not hold
out a context will therefore report strong, meaningless feature utility. We mention it because
we initially drew exactly that wrong conclusion. The warning survives the model correction --- the
regime scale gap is a property of the protocol, not of the activation rule --- but the coefficients
do not.

\paragraph{3. Density does not explain a sign flip, and we no longer know that there is one.}
We would like a crisp graph-level criterion predicting when out-degree fails. We do not have one. The
correlation we once reported between $\langle k \rangle$ and $\rho_{\text{degree}}$ was weak and is
\withdrawn, because both of its components came from rank correlations measured at
$\text{MC} = 12$; a re-measurement at $\text{MC} = 300$ under the corrected activation rule moves
Congress-Twitter from $-0.112$ to $+0.039$ in the same cell, so the sign of the effect is not
established. The structural hint in \S\ref{sec:regime} (in-/out-degree coupling) remains a
hypothesis; nine graphs is not enough to establish it, and it would need to be tested with paired
measurements rather than with correlations whose noise exceeds their signal.

\paragraph{4. Scale and dataset coverage.}
Our largest graph has $15{,}233$ nodes; the source model's datasets are smaller still. We do not
demonstrate behaviour on million-node graphs, and the per-decision cost of obtaining the state
--- one cascade, which is cheap here --- would need re-examination at that scale.

\paragraph{5. The surrogate gap is measured, and it exposes an open question.}
\S\ref{sec:coverage} observes that RR sampling \emph{is} applicable to the monotone upper bound
$\lambda(S) \ge \sigma(S)$ used by the source model: $\sigma^\kappa$ and $\sigma^\tau$ each fix one
threshold per node, so each is monotone submodular and the coverage identity applies to each
individually. What the identity does not reach is their \emph{difference}. We therefore measured
the gap $\lambda(S) - \sigma(S)$ directly, which the source model does not report
(Table~\ref{tab:surrogate}).

\begin{table}[t]

Two things follow. First, the bound is \emph{tight where it is useful}: on Congress-Twitter at
$|S|/n = 10\%$ it over-estimates the objective by $9.8\%$, which makes the surrogate worth
optimising --- and $\sigma^\kappa$ and $\sigma^\tau$ are genuine LT influence functions, so RR
sampling applies to each individually. Second, the gap degrades with saturation and, under our
reading of the definitions, \emph{inverts}: $\lambda < \sigma$ in $4$ of $6$ settings. Across a
wider sweep ($4$ graphs $\times$ $3$ fractions $\times$ $30$ trials) the per-realisation
containment that the source model's proof invokes holds in $0/180$ draws and the inequality
$|A| \le |A^\kappa| - |A^\tau|$ holds in $73/180$.

We deliberately do not present this as a refutation. The source states Theorem~6 for the
expectation of $\sigma$ but proves it on one fixed realisation, and at least three innocent
readings remain open: our interpretation of $\sigma^\kappa/\sigma^\tau$ may differ from the
intended one, the two threshold processes may be intended to be coupled rather than independent,
or the proof may be illustrative rather than general. Every counterexample we observe is a node
active in the $\theta^\tau$ process but not the $\theta^\kappa$ process, which is exactly what a
monotone coupling would forbid. Resolving this requires clarification from the authors, and we
flag it as an open question rather than a result. What we can state without qualification is the
measured gap above, and the structural point that follows: $\lambda$ is a \emph{difference} of two
monotone submodular functions, and submodularity is not preserved under differences, so even a
valid upper bound would not by itself license a sampling-based guarantee.

\paragraph{6. Non-monotonicity is regime-dependent, and we once got this wrong.}
We initially reported that the objective is effectively monotone, based on a sweep at
$|S| \le 4000$ on a graph where that is at most $26\%$ coverage and mostly far less. Measured as
a \emph{fraction}, the objective is clearly non-monotone on most of our graphs
(Table~\ref{tab:signflip}). Any statement about overexposure that does not report $|S|/n$ should
be treated with suspicion --- including, historically, ours.

\paragraph{7. Eight confounds must be removed before any new number is reported.}
Withdrawing the measurements of \S\ref{sec:experiments} is necessary but not sufficient; the
measurement apparatus itself has the following defects, each of which can change a conclusion
independently of the model fix. They are listed here rather than in a private note because a
reviewer cannot verify a number whose protocol is not stated.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Inconsistent stopping under a budget of \emph{at most} $k$.} One policy selects a
        seed with negative marginal gain while another stops at the first non-positive gain. Under
        $\le k$ both are admissible, so the two are not comparable; the stopping rule must be part
        of the policy and stated.
  \item \textbf{Unpriced state acquisition.} Obtaining the exposure vector $\delta_2$ needs one
        cascade, but it is reported at zero cost next to policies that are charged for their
        cascades. The cost column is therefore not a like-for-like comparison.
  \item \textbf{A noisy estimator presented as an oracle.} Greedy at $\text{MC} = 25$ is neither
        exact nor globally optimal and must not be called an oracle; it needs a high-accuracy
        reference with a reported tolerance.
  \item \textbf{Mixed seed sizes in one statistic.} A within/between comparison that pools
        $|S| \in \{0, k, 2k, 3k\}$ is dominated by the $|S| = 0$ cells.
  \item \textbf{Selection bias in the candidate pool.} A single random $30$-node pool makes regime
        selection an uncontrolled variable; the pool must be stratified or the sweep repeated.
  \item \textbf{Splits that do not deduplicate.} Splitting by context identifier allows identical
        seed sets to appear on both sides of a split.
  \item \textbf{Unreported label uncertainty.} Recording $\sigma$ of the label as \texttt{NaN}
        means candidate differences were never compared against estimation error.
  \item \textbf{Weights normalised beyond what the model allows.} In-edge weights are normalised to
        sum to exactly $1$, whereas the model requires at most $1$. Every graph must be reported
        with the normalisation actually used, and the choice must be varied as a sensitivity check.
\end{enumerate}

Two of these interact with the model fix in the same direction: defect 2 charges nothing for the
state that the correction makes more valuable, and defect 8 sets the exposure scale that decides
whether any node is over-exposed at all. A re-derived table that fixes the state machine but not
these eight would not be trustworthy.
