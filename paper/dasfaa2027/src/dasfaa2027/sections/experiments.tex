\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Graphs.}
Table~\ref{tab:signflip} lists the eight graphs. All are loaded with in-edge weights
normalised so that $\sum_{u} \omega_{uv} = 1$ for every $v$ with in-edges. This normalisation is
not a convenience: the model \emph{requires} weights large enough that a plausible number of
active in-neighbours can push $\delta$ into the window. With uniform weights $p = 0.01$ on a
sparse graph, $\delta$ is bounded by $0.01 \times \text{in-degree}$ --- at most $0.6$ and in
practice about $0.15$ on NetHEPT --- so the cascade barely fires (mean marginal gain $1.046$,
i.e.\ essentially the seed itself) and no overexposure is observable at all. The datasets used
by the source model already carry such normalised weights.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Two design choices matter and we state them explicitly because ignoring either produces wrong
conclusions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Measure the seed fraction, not the budget.} As \S\ref{sec:regime} shows, the
        regime is determined by $|S|/n$. Every table reports both.
  \item \textbf{Use paired evaluation with shared random numbers.} We compare policies by
        running them on \emph{the same} threshold windows and reporting the difference in
        spread. Because the process is deterministic given the windows
        (Observation~\ref{obs:setfunction}), this cancels the dominant source of variance and
        resolves marginal differences that an unpaired comparison cannot.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{A measurement caveat that constrains the field}

Before reporting ranking results we must report a trap we fell into. In the regime where the
true marginal is near zero, the \emph{labels themselves} are hard to measure. Table~\ref{tab:snr}
reports the signal-to-noise ratio of paired marginal-gain labels on Congress-Twitter: the ratio
of the spread of the true marginals to the mean standard error of their estimates.

10\% & $250$ & $-0.245$ & $0.338$ & $0.249$ & $1.36$ & $+0.588$ \\
10\% & $800$ & $-0.179$ & $0.349$ & $0.132$ & $\mathbf{2.65}$ & $+0.613$ \\
20\% & $60$  & $-0.018$ & $0.521$ & $0.383$ & $1.36$ & $+0.469$ \\

\bottomrule

At $60$ trials the SNR is below $1.4$: label noise is as large as the dispersion being measured.
An earlier version of our own analysis concluded from such numbers that ``all scorers fail once
saturated''; at $800$ trials the same measurement gives $\rho_{\delta_2} = 0.61$--$0.83$. We
therefore restrict rank-correlation claims in this regime to $\text{MC} \ge 300$, and prefer
paired spread comparisons, which sidestep the issue entirely. We report this because it is
actionable for anyone working on overexposure, and because we have not seen it stated.

\subsection{Main comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares three training-free policies on Congress-Twitter, at a budget of
$10$ additional seeds drawn from a pool of $60$, over $400$ paired trials.

\begin{table}[t]

Degree becomes harmful once saturated; the closed form $\delta_2$ beats both degree and exact
Monte-Carlo greedy.}
\label{tab:main}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
$|S|$ & $|S|/n$ & degree top-$10$ & $\delta_2$ top-$10$ & exact MC-greedy top-$10$ \\
\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Unsaturated, everything is equal.}
At $|S|/n = 0$ the three policies are within $1.5\%$ of one another. Degree is already adequate,
and there is nothing to improve. This is worth stating plainly: the interesting regime is
$|S|/n \ge 5\%$, and work that reports only low-coverage results will find no effect.

\paragraph{Saturated, degree is harmful.}
Adding the ten highest-degree nodes \emph{reduces} the spread by $1.6$ to $5.2$ relative to the
pre-existing seed set. At $|S|/n = 20\%$ the deficit is $-5.17 \pm 0.40$, i.e.\ $13$ standard
errors. A degree heuristic is not merely suboptimal here; it is worse than doing nothing.

\paragraph{Exact greedy also fails, and the closed form wins.}
Exact Monte-Carlo greedy recovers only $+0.42 \pm 0.31$ at $|S|/n = 20\%$, whereas $\delta_2$
recovers $+2.26 \pm 0.24$ --- more than $4$ standard errors better. The mechanism is the one
given in \S\ref{sec:analysis}: the greedy searches for the largest marginal using
$\text{MC} = 25$ estimates while the true marginals are all near zero, so its selection is
noise-driven. The closed form, being deterministic, is not.

\subsection{Comparison against the source model's own algorithms}

The source model evaluates its methods against \texttt{Random}, \texttt{Max\_Degree},
\texttt{IMRank}, \texttt{PageRank} and \texttt{CELF}. Since we are questioning the machinery those
methods avoid, we implement all five, together with the two algorithms the source model actually
proposes: incremental greedy (\textsc{IGA}, its Algorithm 1), which greedily maximises the
objective, and its upper-bound method (\textsc{UB}), which optimises the surrogate
$\lambda(S) = \sigma^\kappa(S) - \sigma^\tau(S)$. Every policy is evaluated on the true objective
under a paired protocol with shared threshold windows.

Four points from Table~\ref{tab:baselines}.

\paragraph{The proposed method does not beat a zero-cost closed form.}
\textsc{IGA} attains a mean marginal of $+132.21$ against $\delta_2$'s $+133.20$, while spending
$13{,}875$ cascade simulations per seed set against $\delta_2$'s zero. Its exact variant
\textsc{CELF} reaches $+132.86$ at the same cost and still falls short. The gap widens sharply in
the regime this paper is about: restricted to $|S|/n \ge 10\%$, $\delta_2$ averages $+8.13$ while
\textsc{IGA} and \textsc{CELF} average $+4.66$ and $+4.74$ --- roughly half, for three orders of
magnitude more computation. On Congress-Twitter at $|S|/n = 10\%$ both are \emph{negative}
($-1.33 \pm 0.80$ and $-0.31 \pm 0.71$) where $\delta_2$ reaches $+6.20 \pm 0.16$. The $\gamma/k$
guarantee the source model proves for \textsc{IGA} is consistent with this: it is a weak bound,
and empirically the method is the wrong tool once the network saturates.

\paragraph{$\delta_2$ is the only policy whose worst case is comfortably positive.}
Every other row has a negative cell in the sweep; \texttt{PageRank} is the sole baseline that
never goes negative, at $+0.24$, but it is second-worst overall.

\paragraph{Optimising the surrogate produces a poor seed set overall.}
The \textsc{UB} arms are the two weakest policies, despite $\sigma^\kappa$ and $\sigma^\tau$ being
genuine monotone submodular influence functions. The mechanics are visible: on Congress-Twitter a
single seed already activates $467$ of $475$ nodes under the lower threshold $\theta^\kappa$, so
greedy on the surrogate stops after one or three seeds because no further seed has positive
marginal gain \emph{under the surrogate} --- while those same seeds leave substantial spread
unrealised under the true objective. Notably \textsc{UB}-$\kappa$ does comparatively well
\emph{inside} the saturated subset ($+5.89$, second only to $\delta_2$), so the surrogate is not
uniformly useless; it is the low-coverage behaviour and the early termination that hurt.
This is the practical face of the gap measured in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\paragraph{The ordering is not explained by cost.}
\texttt{PageRank}, \texttt{Random} and \textsc{IMRank} all spend zero cascades and beat both
\textsc{UB} arms, which also spend zero. What separates the rows is whether a policy reacts to the
overexposure \emph{state}: only $\delta_2$ does, and it is the only one that is never harmful.

\subsection{Robustness across selection draws}

A single selection draw could make $\delta_2$ look good by luck. We therefore repeated the whole
select-then-evaluate procedure over $8$ independent draws, scoring every selection on a shared
set of $100$ evaluation windows so that only the selection varies.

The degree baseline is itself stable across draws ($+5.07$ to $+5.97$ on the $10\%$ block), which
confirms that the degradation it suffers is real rather than a selection artefact.

\paragraph{Stopping.}
Because $\delta_2$ is not calibrated (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}), we pair it with a
\emph{patience} stop on the observed spread rather than on the score. With patience $2$ the
policy stops after $3$ to $6$ seeds and still converts the degree deficit into a gain at every
saturated fraction ($5\%$: $-4.59 \to -0.50$; $10\%$: $-1.97 \to +2.23$; $20\%$: $-5.06 \to +1.00$
over $200$ paired trials), spending one cascade per step.

\subsection{The advantage is graph-dependent}

On NetHEPT the picture is different, and we report it because it bounds the claim.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{NetHEPT ($\langle k \rangle = 4.23$). Degree remains beneficial at every fraction and
actually wins at $|S|/n = 20\%$.}
\label{tab:nethept}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
$|S|/n$ & degree & $\delta_2$ \\
\midrule
 0\% & $+123.45 \pm 5.53$ & $\mathbf{+126.21 \pm 5.58}$ \\
10\% & $+41.80 \pm 2.07$  & $\mathbf{+49.05 \pm 2.00}$ \\
20\% & $\mathbf{+15.70 \pm 0.94}$ & $+11.05 \pm 0.92$ \\

\end{table}

On the sparsest graph, saturation is reached through the \emph{number} of seeds rather than
through any individual node's degree, so degree does not become an anti-signal. The correct
claim is therefore ``in the saturated regime, on graphs where it bites'', not ``degree is
universally harmful''.
